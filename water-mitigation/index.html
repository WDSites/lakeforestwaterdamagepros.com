My wall is wet and I do not know what to do

That sentence describes most mitigation calls. People search for a damp wall, a soft baseboard or a wet ceiling and only later learn the trade term. What you need is someone who can read moisture behind finishes, shut down the source and start controlled drying.

Water mitigation is that process. It combines inspection, partial removal of materials that cannot dry in time, and engineered drying with air movers and dehumidifiers. Once the structure is dry, repair can rebuild it.

When water mitigation is an emergency

Every hour water sits in carpet pad, insulation or drywall, it spreads and the odds of saving the material drop. Mitigation is an emergency service when water is still flowing, when it has soaked more than one room, or when it contacts sewage or outdoor floodwater.

Structural drying, explained

Structural drying is the engineered part of mitigation. Crews place air movers to push air across wet surfaces and dehumidifiers to pull moisture out of the room, and they measure temperature and humidity to confirm the equipment is working.

Low-grain refrigerant dehumidifiers handle most residential jobs, and desiccant units are used in cooler spaces or when deeper drying is needed. Daily readings on the same marked spots show whether materials are trending toward the dry reference, and the equipment stays until they get there. The published industry standard for this work is ANSI/IICRC S500, and homeowners can ask any company whether its crews work to it.

Typical jobs take about three to five days, but that is not a promise. Thick framing, flooring adhesive, insulation and plaster all slow the process, and a house with high indoor humidity takes longer than one with dry air.

Climate notes for Lake Forest drying jobs

Wet-season storm leaks need fast drying because winter air is cooler and damp, while dry summer air can speed drying but also hides slow plumbing leaks.
